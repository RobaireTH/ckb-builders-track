## Key Learnings

- **The Fiber Payroll v1 marks payouts as paid before any Fiber payment has actually happened, and that single fact reframes the whole expansion.** When I traced the executor, I found that `packages/mcp/src/payroll/executor.ts` drives the four engine calls in sequence and then, right after `complete_job` returns, sets `status: 'paid'` and `fiberSettlementStatus: 'paid'` (lines 86 and 92). The thing it never does is call the Fiber transfer. The real settlement machinery lives one layer over, in the MCP `POST /jobs/:tx_hash/:index/complete` route, which parses the `NERVE_PAYMENT` metadata embedded in the job description and then fires `payAgentByLockArgs` or the hold-invoice settlement. The payroll executor embeds that same `NERVE_PAYMENT` blob into the description (line 61) but reaches core directly and skips the route that would read it. So the on-chain job lifecycle is honest, the ledger says the contractor was paid, and no Fiber payment was attempted. 

- **Almost everything I want to expand `mitosis` into is already half-wired, which changes the kind of work the next few weeks should be.** The Fiber layer in `packages/mcp/src/fiber.ts` already exposes `newInvoice`, `newHoldInvoice`, `settleInvoice`, `sendPayment`, `openChannel`, and a readiness probe. The `routes/fiber.ts` file already resolves a contractor's lock args to a Fiber node through a small file-backed map (`.fiber-agents.json`), and `payAgentByLockArgs` already handles the awkward case where no channel exists by opening a 100 CKB channel, waiting for it to become ready, and retrying. The expansion is less about inventing capability and more about routing the payroll product through capability that is sitting there unused. The honest framing I landed on is that the engine is ahead of the product surface, and the product surface is what needs to catch up.

- **UDT and stablecoin payouts are a configuration seam, not a rebuild.** In `fiber.ts`, `openChannel` currently hardcodes `funding_udt_type_script: null`, which means every channel is CKB only. The SDK already accepts a UDT type script in that slot. For a payroll product this matters more than it looks: a contractor who agrees to a number this Monday and gets paid next Monday is exposed to CKB price movement in between, and a USD-pegged UDT removes that for them. I agreed this is one of the few expansions that is genuinely product-defining rather than infrastructure for its own sake, because it speaks to the contractor's lived experience of getting paid.

- **The `job_cell` contract gives the payroll product a real settlement guarantee, and I had been undervaluing that inheritance.** Reading `contracts/src/bin/job_cell.rs` again, the state machine enforces `Open -> Reserved -> Claimed -> Completed` and rejects any other transition with `ERR_INVALID_STATUS`. The piece I want to lean on for payroll is `verify_settlement_outputs`: when a claimed job cell is destroyed, the transaction has to produce outputs locked to the worker's lock args that sum to at least the reward, or it fails with `ERR_WORKER_UNDERPAID`. That is a contractor-protection property baked into the chain, and a payroll product can advertise it honestly. The work is to stop hiding it behind a status field that lies about Fiber and start surfacing the on-chain proof that the engine already produces.

- **The thing that makes `pckt` safe is that the lock trusts almost nothing off-chain, and the recent hardening was mostly about making the backend stop pretending it could be trusted.** The lock in `contract/pckt-lock/src/main.rs` is the only custody anchor. Funds move in exactly two ways: a valid claim signature, or an owner reclaim after expiry. The backend never holds keys and never authorizes a spend. What the recent commits did was tighten the backend's role as an indexer and relay so it cannot be used to poison the metadata that the on-chain story leans on. The relay now refuses any transaction whose `cell_deps` do not reference the configured packet lock (`relay.rs`), the message store now recomputes `blake160(body)` and rejects a mismatch against the claimed `message_hash` (`messages.rs`), and profile upsert decodes `sender_address` and requires it to equal the claimed `owner_lock_hash` (`profiles.rs`). None of these protect funds, because funds were never at risk through the backend. They protect the integrity of what users see.

- **The claim signature binds the recipient into the signed message, and that one detail is what makes front-running a non-issue.** In `verify_claim_signature`, the message that gets signed is `blake2b("ckb-default-hash", input_outpoint || claimer_lock_hash)`, and the lock recovers the public key from the 65-byte recoverable signature and requires it to equal the packet's `claim_pubkey`. Because the claimer's own lock hash is inside the signed bytes, a signature is only ever valid for the exact recipient it names. Someone watching the mempool cannot lift the signature and redirect the payout to themselves, because the moment they change the recipient the signature stops verifying. Double claiming is blocked separately by scanning `claimed_locks` for the claimer and returning `AlreadyClaimed` (error 55). A malicious relayer can delay or drop a claim, which is griefing, and it cannot steal, which is the property that actually matters.

- **The lucky-money randomness is fair but fully predictable, and naming that honestly was more useful than pretending it was a flaw.** Each slot's payout comes from `slot_seed = blake2b(salt || slot_index)` truncated to eight bytes, then `payout = (seed % range) + floor`, with a reserve that keeps at least 63 CKB (`MIN_SLOT_SHANNONS`) available for every remaining slot. The salt lives in the public cell data. That means anyone can compute every slot's payout before claiming, so a claimer can see which slot pays the most and time their claim accordingly. The sender cannot pre-position against recipients, so it is fair in the sense that matters for a gift, but it is not unpredictable, and there is no commit-reveal scheme to make it so. This is a deliberate trade-off rather than a fund-safety bug, and writing it down as a known property is better than discovering it later as a surprise.

## Practical Progress

### mitosis: mapping the expansion
- Walked the full payout path end to end and confirmed where it diverges from the engine's own settlement path. The payroll executor at `packages/mcp/src/payroll/executor.ts` calls `POST /tx/build-and-broadcast` four times (`post_job`, `reserve_job`, `claim_job`, `complete_job`) and then marks the payout `paid` and `fiberSettlementStatus: 'paid'`. It never calls the Fiber transfer, and it never goes through `POST /jobs/:tx_hash/:index/complete`, which is the only place that reads `NERVE_PAYMENT` metadata and triggers `payAgentByLockArgs` or hold-invoice settlement.
- Confirmed the settlement machinery that the executor should be using is already built: `packages/mcp/src/fiber.ts` for the SDK primitives, and `packages/mcp/src/routes/fiber.ts` for the lock-args-to-node resolution, the auto-channel-open fallback, and the hold-invoice pay and settle functions.
- Confirmed the contractor-protection guarantee the product can honestly advertise: `verify_settlement_outputs` in `contracts/src/bin/job_cell.rs` requires outputs to the worker's lock args summing to at least the reward, failing with `ERR_WORKER_UNDERPAID` otherwise.
- Identified the UDT/stablecoin seam: `openChannel` in `packages/mcp/src/fiber.ts` hardcodes `funding_udt_type_script: null`, and the SDK accepts a UDT type script there. This is the cheapest path to USD-pegged payouts.
- Reviewed the storage boundary for a future database migration: `packages/mcp/src/payroll/store.ts` is a single whole-file JSON read and write with no locking and no indexes, behind a `PayrollStore` shape that was deliberately built to be swapped for SQLite or Postgres later. Multi-employer org accounts and real auth attach naturally at the same boundary, since the portal today is only an unguessable UUID token in `portal.ts` with no scoping.

### mitosis: the expansion list I came out of the week with
- **Wire real Fiber settlement.** Route payout completion through the existing Fiber functions so `fiberSettlementStatus` reflects an actual payment, and so the hold-invoice path can back a fund-now, release-on-approval escrow.
- **UDT and stablecoin payouts.** Enable UDT-funded channels via the existing `funding_udt_type_script` slot so contractors can be paid in a stable unit.
- **Recurring and scheduled payouts.** A small scheduler over the existing `createPayout` and `execute` calls turns one-off payouts into payroll cycles.
- **Streaming pay over Fiber channels.** Per-period micropayment streaming maps onto by-the-hour contractor work, which on-chain transfers cannot do affordably.
- **Real database and multi-employer accounts.** Swap the JSON store for a real backend at the `PayrollStore` boundary and add tenant scoping plus authentication, using the portal token as the seam.
- **On-chain proof in the ledger.** `GET /payroll/ledger` already normalizes the four transaction hashes per payout; extend it to a CSV export with links to the on-chain settlement so payroll is auditable and tax-exportable.
- **Approval thresholds.** Use the existing SupeRISE signer backend in `packages/core` toward employer multi-signature approval before a payout executes.

### pckt: security model review
- Read the lock contract top to bottom and wrote out the trust boundary in plain terms: the lock trusts whoever holds the off-chain claim secret and trusts the witness-supplied `claimer_lock_hash`, and it trusts the backend for nothing. Custody is fully on-chain.
- Confirmed the claim path's guarantees: single group input enforced (a second input is rejected), invariants checked in `enforce_invariants` (version 1, `packet_type` at most 3, `1 <= slots_total <= 64`, `slots_claimed == claimed_locks.len()`, message at most 256 bytes), double-claim blocked via `claimed_locks` scan (`AlreadyClaimed`, 55), and the signed message binding the recipient's lock hash so a claim cannot be redirected.
- Confirmed the recipient-cell tightening that recent commits added: `verify_recipient` now requires the claimer's output to carry no type script and empty data, returning `RecipientHasData` (75) otherwise. This was audit item C6.
- Confirmed the successor conservation: for a non-final slot, exactly one output returns to the same lock, byte-identical except that `slots_claimed` increments and the new claimer is appended to `claimed_locks`, with `succ_cap = input_cap - payout` computed through `checked_sub` so capacity is conserved. The final slot forbids a successor.
- Confirmed the sender's refund route: `reclaim_path` requires an absolute-timestamp `since` at or beyond `expiry` (`ReclaimBeforeExpiry`, 80), requires an input owned by `owner_lock_hash`, and forbids a successor. This is the 7-day expiry refund.
- Walked the backend hardening commit by commit and mapped each to the consolidated audit: relay now requires the packet-lock `cell_dep` reference, messages verify `blake160(body)` against `message_hash` (audit H7), profiles bind `sender_address` to `owner_lock_hash` (audit C1), and shortlinks moved to a CSPRNG slug with a host allow-list (audit C2 and C3).
- Read the reorg handling in `backend/src/indexer/runner.rs`: `detect_reorg` compares the new block's `parent_hash` against the stored hash at the previous height, and on a mismatch rolls back `REORG_DEPTH` (50) blocks and re-runs the backfill to restore live packet rows. The important caveat is that a reorg deeper than 50 blocks is not detected, and the indexer is purely informational, so fund safety never depends on it.

### pckt: open security edges I am carrying forward
- **Claim secret in the URL fragment (C5).** The private claim key lives in the link fragment, and the product encourages sharing the link. Browser sync, extensions, screenshots, and chat-app link previews can all observe a fragment. Some mitigations have landed (a no-referrer policy, fragment stripping on mailto), but the reveal-on-tap masking is not fully in place. This is the realistic theft surface, and it is a UX problem on top of a sound cryptographic core.
- **Rate limiter trusts `X-Forwarded-For` (C4).** The per-IP map is keyed on a header that any client can set, and the map is never evicted, so a rotating header value grows memory without bound. Until this is fixed the per-IP limits are cosmetic.
- **No domain separator in the claim signature (H3).** The signed message has no chain id and no protocol tag. It is fine in isolation, and it would become a real defense-in-depth gap if a per-claim secret were ever reused across protocols.
- **Successor floor not re-checked after a lucky payout (H2).** `succ_cap` is computed but not re-validated against the successor's required data floor, which could in principle leave an under-funded successor that bricks later claims for an under-funded lucky packet.

## Reflections
The honest shape of the week is that I learned more from reading my own code with a specific person's questions in my ear than I would have from writing new code without them. On `mitosis`, the discovery that the v1 reports a Fiber settlement it never performs could have felt like a setback. It became the opposite, because it turned a vague sense of "the product needs to do more" into one concrete first task with the plumbing already present. The engine is genuinely ahead of the product, and the expansion is mostly the work of letting the product use what the engine can already do, in an order that respects the contractor's actual experience: get the real payment firing, then remove their price exposure with a stable unit, then make payouts repeat on a schedule.

On `pckt`, the reflection is more reassuring. The security model holds where it has to hold. Custody is on-chain, the lock trusts nothing it should not, and the recent commit stream did real work closing the audit's critical and high backend findings. What is left is mostly the soft surface around the hard core: a claim secret that is cryptographically sound but lives somewhere users can leak it, a rate limiter built on an honor system, and a signature that could use a domain tag for future-proofing. Writing those down as named, accepted edges rather than vague worries is the point of the week. I would rather carry a precise list than a feeling.

## Challenges
- The hardest part of the `mitosis` review was trusting the code over my own memory of what I thought I had built. I went in believing the payroll path settled over Fiber, and it took reading the executor line by line to accept that it does not. The lesson I am keeping is that a status field is a claim the code makes about itself, and a claim is worth verifying against the actual call graph before I quote it to anyone, including Arthur.
- Separating deliberate v1 deferral from accidental gaps was slower than expected. The design spec lists recurring payroll, tax, and multi-tenancy as out of scope on purpose, while the missing Fiber settlement is an accidental gap that reads almost identically from the outside. Getting that distinction right mattered, because one is a roadmap item and the other is a correctness bug wearing the same clothes.
- On `pckt`, the temptation was to treat every open audit item with equal weight. The useful discipline was ranking them by what they actually threaten. The claim-secret leakage surface is the one that can cost a user money, so it sits above the missing domain separator, which is purely future-proofing. Holding that ordering instead of flattening the list into "things to fix" is what makes the security model legible rather than just long.
- Running a direction week with no commits always feels thinner than it is. The artifact that justifies the week is this account and the expansion list, not a green contribution graph, and I had to remind myself that a wrong settlement design shipped fast would have cost far more than a week spent understanding the right one.
